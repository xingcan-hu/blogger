# Topic scoring and output

## Scoring rubric

Score each dimension from 1 to 5, then calculate a weighted score out of 100.

| Dimension | Weight | Question |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Search evidence | 25 | How strongly do observed queries and result patterns support the topic? |
| Audience pain | 20 | Is there a clear, repeated problem or desired outcome? |
| Product fit | 20 | Can the product solve the problem naturally without forcing a promotion? |
| Differentiation | 15 | Can the article add a test, example, comparison, or missing angle? |
| Conversion proximity | 10 | Is the reader close to trying a relevant tool? |
| Freshness or social momentum | 10 | Is there a current, seasonal, or discussion-driven hook? |

Formula: multiply each 1–5 rating by its weight, add the results, and divide by 5. Round to the nearest whole number.

Interpretation:

- 80–100: publish or test first;
- 65–79: strong supporting topic;
- 50–64: keep in backlog or strengthen the evidence;
- below 50: merge, reframe, or drop.

Do not let the numeric score hide weak evidence. Mark a score as provisional when search or social research is incomplete.

## Minimum topic table

| Priority | Proposed title | Primary keyword | Intent | Evidence | Product bridge | Freshness | Effort | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |

For `Evidence`, summarize the signal and link its source. For `Product bridge`, state exactly where the product belongs in the article. Use `evergreen`, `seasonal`, or `timely` for freshness. Use `low`, `medium`, or `high` effort.

## Top-three brief

For each winning topic provide:

- working title;
- primary keyword and two to five secondary phrases;
- reader and search intent;
- why now;
- unique evidence or test to produce;
- four to seven section outline;
- natural call to action;
- internal-link opportunities;
- claims or risks that require verification.

## Source log

Keep a compact source log when research is extensive:

| Source | Type | Date checked | Signal or fact | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use `high` for direct first-party facts, `medium` for repeated public observations, and `low` for a single anecdote or inference.
